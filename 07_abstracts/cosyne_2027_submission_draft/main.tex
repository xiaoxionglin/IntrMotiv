\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Spatial representations emerge from temporal event credit in a hippocampus-inspired navigation agent without spatial supervision}\par
\end{center}
\vspace{-5pt}

Can spatial representations emerge from sensory inputs and learning objectives that do not explicitly encode space? We investigate this question using a bottom-up computational architecture inspired by hippocampal physiology, exploring whether spatial organization can arise from the temporal structure of sensory experience and self-generated behavior, without position labels, maps, or spatial rewards.

Our model combines dentate gyrus (DG)-like sparse sensory events with CA3-like sequential memory. Temporal relationships between encountered DG events provide two complementary learning signals: a \textbf{``push''} that reinforces DG activity more strongly after longer intervals between events, and a \textbf{``pull''} that rewards shorter transitions between sensory events or, in goal-conditioned variants, faster arrival at internally defined DG targets. Together, these pressures aim to make temporal separation a more informative proxy for navigational distance. The agent learns from egocentric visual experience and intrinsic motivations, without explicit information about its physical location or spatial distances.

During exploration of a continuous visual environment, spatially informative DG activity emerges. To isolate the contribution of temporal information, we compare temporally weighted DG feedback against mean-matched constant feedback, evaluating the resulting representations on identical sensory observations. Temporal feedback produces higher DG spatial information, less redundant response maps, and a broader spatial distribution of field peaks across five paired training seeds. These improvements extend to downstream decoder representations, where spatial information also increases consistently. In contrast, temporally weighting the policy's target-hit reward does not consistently improve exploration or spatial representation quality compared with constant hit rewards.

The agent's behavior further shapes spatial coding. Even with network weights frozen, the learned policy produces more spatially informative downstream activity than random actions, demonstrating that spatial coding depends not only on the learned network but also on how the agent interacts with its environment.

Together, our results demonstrate how spatial coding can emerge from the interaction of temporal learning, sequential memory, and self-generated behavior, without explicitly optimizing for spatial information.

\textbf{Model and learning rules.} We ask whether an agent can acquire spatially informative neural activity when its learning signals refer to sensory events and time, rather than physical location. A fixed pretrained visual trunk feeds a trainable, sparsified DG layer. Qualifying DG event onsets enter a fixed CA3-like sequence memory, giving the policy access to the recent order of sensory events (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). The controller additionally receives egocentric depth, but not allocentric position, an externally supplied map, or a spatial reward; physical coordinates are used only afterward to evaluate spatial coding. The \emph{push} reinforces DG activity at an event more strongly when the preceding eligible event occurred further in the past. This is credit weighted by elapsed decisions, not an explicit spatial-separation loss. The \emph{pull} rewards shorter transitions: the baseline policy receives intrinsic reward for short intervals between events, whereas goal-conditioned policies receive a bonus when an internally selected DG target is activated. A frontier variant changes how those targets are chosen. Movement may make temporal separation informative about navigational distance, although loops and repeated views can violate this relationship.

\textbf{Three complementary tests.} We distinguish spatial information measured along an evolving agent's trajectories, spatial information generated by different actions with network weights frozen, and differences between separately trained networks evaluated on the same sensory observations. These tests ask, respectively, whether spatial coding appears during exploration, whether behavior changes the coding of a fixed network, and whether temporal DG credit changes the learned representation.

\textbf{Spatial coding during exploration (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}A).} In all five training seeds of each tested policy family, the online DG spatial-information measure rises from early to late training. Because the measure uses observations sampled by each agent's evolving policy, it reflects both neural responses and the experiences collected by that policy. The increasing online information therefore documents spatially informative activity during exploration, but does not by itself isolate a change in DG tuning on a fixed set of inputs.

\textbf{Behavior-dependent spatial coding (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}B).} To test the role of behavior directly, we freeze each trained network and compare its own policy with uniform random actions. The learned policy yields higher spatial information per activation in the first decoder hidden layer in all 38 tested models, after restricting the analysis to shared spatial bins and weighting those bins equally. The action policies encounter different views and histories of DG events, which produce different CA3 states even when all network parameters are unchanged. The result demonstrates that spatial coding depends on self-generated behavior, not only on fixed synaptic weights. Split-half map reliability generally favors the learned policy under the main analysis criterion, although that comparison is sensitive to occupancy thresholds. This intervention does not show that behavior changed DG weights during training.

\textbf{Temporal event credit organizes the DG code (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}C,D).} We compare temporal DG credit with a positive constant credit calibrated to the same mean magnitude, retaining the same eligibility rule for rewarded event onsets. To avoid confusing different evaluation trajectories with differences in the learned code, we replay each trained network on the identical sequence of 10,001 saved observations at approximately 100 million training frames. In the baseline policy, temporal rather than constant credit raises mean DG spatial information from $0.071$ to $0.128$ (paired difference $+0.0575$), lowers active-unit response-map cosine from $0.080$ to $0.047$, and broadens the effective spatial area covered by DG peak locations from $0.285$ to $0.394$. All three effects have the same direction across all five paired seeds. The map-cosine and peak-distribution changes indicate that the effect goes beyond simply amplifying DG responses: the units become less redundant and their peaks are more widely distributed. Panel C illustrates the seed with the largest peak-area difference, whereas panel D includes all matched pairs. Goal-conditioned variants show some similar changes but are less consistent; the frontier condition currently has only three matched encoder pairs. The shared replay holds evaluation sampling fixed, not the experiences encountered during training.

\textbf{Spatial information reaches downstream layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}E).} On the same saved input sequence, temporal DG credit also increases decoder-1 and decoder-2 spatial information per activation in all five baseline seed pairs, by $+0.680$ and $+0.636$ bits on average, respectively. The effect is therefore not confined to DG activity: spatial information is also available in the learned downstream policy readout. The CA3 contrast is more dependent on the normalization of neural activity, so we do not claim a uniform improvement across every layer.

\textbf{What does temporal weighting of the pull add? (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}F).} In the goal-conditioned policy, both a temporal and a mean-matched constant target-hit bonus reward activation of the selected DG detector; ordinary actor--critic discounting also favors earlier rewards. A five-seed factorial comparison crosses these worker-bonus conditions with temporal versus constant DG credit. With DG credit held fixed, a temporal instead of constant hit bonus has no consistent effect on exploration: mean visited-area differences are $+0.026$ with temporal DG credit and $-0.087$ with constant DG credit. Effects on DG and downstream spatial information are likewise mixed or unfavorable. This comparison tests the added \emph{timing} of the worker bonus, not the presence or absence of the pull itself. Moreover, hitting a learned DG detector does not establish arrival at an independently defined physical goal; reliable commanded navigation has yet to be demonstrated.

\textbf{Interpretation.} These results illustrate a constructive route by which temporally structured sensory experience can organize a spatial code without a spatial learning objective. Temporal event credit makes the learned DG responses more spatially informative and less redundant, with corresponding gains in downstream decoder activity. Self-generated behavior also changes the spatial information carried by a fixed network. Thus temporal learning, sequential memory, and behavior can contribute to spatial coding through distinct mechanisms, even though the additional benefit of temporally weighted goal reward and the controllability of individual learned landmarks remain open questions.
