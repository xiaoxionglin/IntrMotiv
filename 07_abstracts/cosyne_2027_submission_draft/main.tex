\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Spatial representations emerge from temporal event credit in a hippocampus-inspired navigation agent without spatial supervision}\par
\end{center}
\vspace{-5pt}

Can spatial representations emerge from sensory inputs and learning objectives that do not explicitly encode space? We investigate this question using a bottom-up computational architecture inspired by hippocampal physiology, exploring whether spatial organization can arise from the temporal structure of sensory experience and self-generated behavior, without position labels, maps, or spatial rewards.

Our model combines dentate gyrus (DG)-like sparse sensory events with CA3-like sequential memory. Temporal relationships between encountered DG events provide two complementary learning signals: a \textbf{``push''} that reinforces DG activity more strongly after longer intervals between events, and a \textbf{``pull''} that rewards shorter transitions between sensory events or, in goal-conditioned variants, faster arrival at internally defined DG targets. Together, these pressures aim to make temporal separation a more informative proxy for navigational distance. The agent learns from egocentric visual experience and intrinsic motivations, without explicit information about its physical location or spatial distances.

During exploration of a continuous visual environment, spatially informative DG activity emerges. To isolate the contribution of temporal information, we compare temporally weighted DG feedback against mean-matched constant feedback, evaluating the resulting representations on identical sensory observations. Temporal feedback increases DG spatial information in all five no-goal seed pairs, reduces map overlap in all 15 no-goal and goal-conditioned pairs, and broadens eligible field-peak coverage in 13 of 15. In contrast, temporally weighting the policy's target-hit reward has mixed effects on DG spatial measures in a separate goal-conditioned comparison.

The agent's behavior further shapes spatial coding. With network weights frozen, learned actions produce more informative DG activity in 36 of 38 models and more reliable DG maps in 37 of 38, showing that the expressed code depends on how the agent interacts with its environment.

Together, our results demonstrate how spatial coding can emerge from the interaction of temporal learning, sequential memory, and self-generated behavior, without explicitly optimizing for spatial information.

\textbf{Architecture and learning signals.} Egocentric RGB images pass through an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 trunk fixed through layer 2. A trainable projection, batch normalization, and thresholded rectification then produce sparse dentate gyrus (DG) events. A fixed CA3-like shift memory retains their recent sequence. Its flattened state, together with egocentric depth, feeds a learned two-layer, 128-unit multilayer perceptron (MLP) readout that estimates value and selects among five actions. Temporal DG credit reinforces eligible event responses according to the elapsed number of decisions since a preceding event (push). Policy reward favors shorter event transitions or, in goal-conditioned variants, earlier detection of an internally selected DG target (pull). NG explores without a goal, UG selects DG targets uniformly, and FG selects frontier DG targets. The agent receives no position coordinates, map, or spatial reward during learning (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}A,B).

\textbf{Three tests.} We track DG coding during training, compare learned and random actions with each network frozen, and vary temporal DG or policy credit across matched training seeds (Figs.~\ref{fig:architecture}B and~\ref{fig:results}). Identical-observation replay isolates a learned-network difference for the DG-credit comparison; the action and policy-credit comparisons use separate trajectories and therefore include evaluation-sampling effects.

\textbf{Spatial emergence and temporal push.} Online DG spatial information rises during training in NG, UG, and FG (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}B); selected FG response maps illustrate the resulting spatial activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}C). Terminal trajectories and path efficiency also differ across the three designs (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}A), so their online scores reflect both network and behavior changes. To isolate the effect of temporal DG credit, we replay the same 10,001 observations through each temporal-credit and mean-matched constant-credit network within a training-seed pair (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}C). Temporal credit increases DG spatial information in all five NG pairs (mean $+0.0575$ bits per decision), four of five UG pairs, and three of five FG pairs. It reduces DG map overlap in all 15 pairs and broadens eligible field-peak coverage in 13 of 15. Reliable stored-graph edge counts vary across seeds; the representation gains do not establish improved goal control.

\textbf{Behavior shapes spatial coding.} With network weights frozen, learned actions elicit higher DG spatial information than uniform random actions in 36 of 38 terminal models and higher DG split-half map reliability in 37 of 38 (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}B). This intervention shows that the expressed spatial code depends on behavior at evaluation. Because the policies visit different observations, it does not isolate a fixed-input representation change or show how behavior shaped the network during training.

\textbf{Temporal pull.} We next vary temporal versus constant target-hit credit while holding temporal DG credit fixed in UG and FG (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}D). At shared PPO discount $\gamma=0.999$ and GAE $\lambda=0.995$, five matched seeds per design show mixed changes in DG spatial information, map overlap, and field-peak coverage. FG gains reliable stored-graph edges in all five pairs, whereas UG edge changes are mixed. These readouts come from separate policy-driven trajectories, so they do not isolate a fixed-input DG change. Stored edges describe detector-memory structure, not physical goal arrival. The pull comparison also uses a different PPO-credit regimen from the push comparison; their effect sizes should not be compared directly.
